The Number on the Brochure Is Not the Number You Bank

A producer's honest read of the Hungary, UK, and Ireland film rebates — and the gap between the headline percentage and what actually lands in your account.

The percentages don't tell the whole story. If you see Hungary's 30%, the UK's 34%, Ireland's 32% rebate — don't pencil it in.

By the time the cost report lands and the actual rebate reconciles against what was modelled, the gap can be ten or fifteen percent of the line. Sometimes more.

That gap is rarely the result of a producer doing something wrong. It's the difference between what the rebate is advertised as and what the rebate actually pays — once you've layered in eligibility rules, qualifying-spend definitions, corporation tax interactions, payment cycle realities, and procedural friction.

This piece walks through what that gap actually looks like in three of the most-compared European jurisdictions — Hungary, the UK, and Ireland — and what to do about it before you anchor a budget to a number you read on a brochure.

Hungary: closest to its headline, until your above-the-line gets expensive

Hungary's headline rebate is 30% on Hungarian eligible spend, extendable to an effective 37.5% by adding up to 25% non-Hungarian costs into the qualifying base. The mechanic is a cash refund, paid via the National Film Institute's collection account after the production period closes and costs have been audited.

In practice, this is the rebate that lands closest to its headline number — if your budget composition cooperates.

Where it shrinks: foreign cast and crew remuneration is 100% eligible only up to HUF 3 million (roughly USD 8,000) per person per film. Anything above that figure is only 50% eligible. A 2025 amendment also changed how those above-the-cap costs feed the foreign qualifying spend base, quietly trimming the effective rate on high-earning above-the-line.

The practical effect: a USD 1M star fee on a USD 5M film shaves your blended effective rate by a meaningful amount. The 37.5% becomes something materially lower, depending on the rest of the composition. The brochure number was correct. Your spreadsheet just didn't price in the cap.

A second layer of friction sits in the timing. A rule that came in for 2026 requires principal photography to begin within six months of registration approval, or the National Film Office can amend or revoke the decision. Pass twelve months without shooting and it's revoked outright. So a producer racing to file ahead of the registration cap can — paradoxically — trigger a clock that ends the very approval they secured.

And finally, the rebate is post-financing. You spend, then audit, then receive. Payout cycles have stretched in recent years. If your plan was to use the rebate to fund the shoot itself, that's not a plan — it's a gap that needs bridging through interim financing or a discount facility against the future rebate.

The productions that come out clean in Hungary are the ones who have the Hungarian side standing up before they register:

The Hungarian production company formed and registered, not "in progress."

A local production partner who actually knows the Film Office, not a name still being chased.

Accounting set up to Hungarian rules from day one, because cost verification has to be audit-ready and filed within a year of wrap.

Financing structured around the fact that the rebate arrives after the spend, not before.

Put those pieces in place ahead of filing, and the six-month clock stops being a threat. It becomes a schedule you were always going to hit.

United Kingdom (AVEC): the biggest paper-to-bank gap

The UK's Audio-Visual Expenditure Credit (AVEC), which replaced the old Film Tax Relief regime for new productions from April 2025, is structurally different in a way that produces the largest gap in this comparison.

AVEC is a taxable expenditure credit, not a cash rebate. The headline 34% rate on qualifying UK expenditure is gross. After corporation tax interaction, the net rate to the production company is approximately 25.5%.

There are higher bands. A 39% rate on qualifying UK VFX spend (with the 80% qualifying-expenditure cap lifted on VFX from 2025) brings the net to roughly 29.25%. An enhanced 53% rate under the Independent Film Tax Credit (IFTC) is available for qualifying films under £15 million of qualifying expenditure with a UK lead director or screenwriter, or as a qualifying co-production — net rate around 39.75%.

For most international productions shooting in the UK, however — the bracket between £20M and £100M+ that doesn't qualify for IFTC — the working number is ~25.5%, not 34%. A producer modelling the headline rate is over-stating their rebate by close to ten points of qualifying spend.

This isn't a criticism of the UK system, which has real advantages: depth of crew, world-class post, English-language workflow, and a studio base that very few jurisdictions can match. It's a flag that the headline-to-bankable conversion is the steepest among the three jurisdictions and needs to be modelled correctly from the outset.

Ireland (Section 481): closer to its headline, gated procedurally

Ireland's Section 481 is a 32% payable tax credit on eligible Irish expenditure — including cast and crew working in Ireland and goods and services sourced there. Because it's payable rather than taxable, the headline-to-bankable gap is smaller than the UK's.

There are two relevant uplifts. The Scéal Uplift, in force from 2025, takes the rate to 40% for qualifying feature films under €20 million of qualifying expenditure, with at least one EEA national in a key creative role. A separate VFX uplift, EU-approved in 2026, takes VFX-heavy productions to a 40% rate where there is at least €1 million of qualifying VFX expenditure.

Where Ireland costs you isn't on the rate — it's on the gating. Productions need a cultural certificate from the Department of Culture, the producer-company structure has specific Irish-residency and co-production requirements, and the Skills Development Plan adds procedural overhead. None of this is prohibitive, but it adds weeks of paperwork and frequently shapes how a project is structured rather than the other way around.

For productions where the creative team and producer-company structure can comfortably meet Section 481's criteria, Ireland sits closest to its headline of the three. For productions where the cultural test or co-production requirements force structural changes, the rate advantage gets eaten by complexity.

The honest comparison

If you ranked the three jurisdictions on how close you actually bank to the headline:

Hungary is closest, until your above-the-line gets expensive and the cast/crew cap starts compressing your effective rate.

Ireland sits closer to its 32% headline than the UK does to its 34%, but the procedural gating shapes the project before the rate even applies.

The UK has the largest paper-to-bank gap on a standard international shoot — ~25.5% net against a 34% headline — though the IFTC and VFX uplift change that picture meaningfully for productions that qualify.

This ranking holds for a generic mid-budget international production. It changes — sometimes substantially — as soon as you layer in specifics: above-the-line composition, VFX-heavy versus practical-heavy, indie versus mid-major budget, co-production structure.

Which is the actual point.

What to do with this

Two things, in this order.

Read the eligible-cost rules before you read the marketing copy. Every rebate jurisdiction publishes its eligibility rules in detail. The provisions that move the needle — caps on cast and crew above a threshold, non-eligible cost categories, qualifying-expenditure definitions, payment cycle timing — are in those documents, not in the country's promotional brochures. The brochures will tell you what the rebate is worth in theory. The eligibility rules will tell you what it's worth in your specific case.

Talk to a producer who's actually closed productions in the jurisdiction. Someone who's processed cost verifications, dealt with the relevant film office, and seen how decisions get reconciled will tell you in twenty minutes what would take you twenty hours of reading to figure out. The mechanics that move the needle aren't in the marketing.

Hungary, the UK, and Ireland are all good jurisdictions to shoot in. The case for each is real. The case against each is also real — and it's the producer's job to model both honestly before the budget is locked.

If Hungary specifically is on your shortlist, mapping the sequence — registration timing, cast composition, financing structure — against your actual numbers is most of what I do on a strategy call.
